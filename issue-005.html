What an Agent Is, and Why It Is Not You

A note on the new identity layer that no one is ready for.

In the last year, I have started to see versions of the same incident across multiple client environments. A user authorizes an AI assistant to handle some category of work on their behalf — scheduling, drafting, vendor coordination, light operational tasks. The assistant does the work. The work goes out signed in the user's name. The systems that receive it authenticate the user correctly. The vendors on the other end trust the messages correctly. The assistant does exactly what it was told to do.

The systems are also, in a way no one in the chain notices, no longer authenticating the user.

This is the identity layer problem of 2026, and it is going to break a lot of security programs before it gets resolved.

What an agent actually is

In the cybersecurity literature, an agent has historically meant either a piece of software running on an endpoint (an endpoint detection agent, a configuration agent) or a human user acting on behalf of an organization (an authorized agent in a legal sense). The new AI agent is neither and both. It is a piece of software that acts on behalf of a human, with the human's authority but not the human's judgment, and increasingly without the human's direct supervision in the loop.

The frameworks that govern most enterprise identity programs have not yet settled what authentication and identification look like for this kind of actor. Most organizations are bolting their AI agents onto existing identity infrastructure that was designed for human users. The agent gets an OAuth token, or a service account, or a delegated credential, and the infrastructure treats it as if it were a human signed in from a slightly unusual location.

It is not a human. It is closer to a long-running automation than to a user, but unlike traditional automation, it makes decisions that look human and produces output that reads human. The accountability question — who is responsible when the agent makes a consequential mistake — does not have a clean answer in the current frameworks.

Three problems your identity layer cannot solve yet

One. Authentication-as-delegation breaks at scale. When an AI assistant sends an email signed in a user's name, what was actually authenticated was a token issued to the assistant at setup, refreshed by an automated process, and revocable only through a flow that the IT team has not yet built. The chain from the email signature back to the user's actual identity passes through three or four systems, each of which trusts the previous one. None of those systems can answer the question: did this person authorize this specific email?

Two. Identification by humans collapses entirely. When the recipient reads the email, they make an identification judgment — is this really my contact writing this? — using the same cognitive shortcuts I wrote about in The Sender Field. A human reading an email that sounds like Khadijat, written in Khadijat's style, signed in Khadijat's name, will identify it as from Khadijat. The identification will be correct in the sense that Khadijat's assistant did write it on her authority. The identification will also be wrong, in the sense that Khadijat herself never read or composed the words. Both of those things are true at once, and the existing identity infrastructure does not give you any way to distinguish between them.

Three. Accountability fractures across the agent stack. If the agent makes a mistake — schedules a meeting it should not have, sends a message to the wrong person, makes a commitment the user did not approve — who is accountable? The user, whose name was on it? The vendor that built the assistant? The IT team that provisioned the integration? The model provider whose weights produced the output? Every answer is partial, and the legal and contractual frameworks have not caught up.

Three things to do this quarter

I will tell you what I am telling my clients.

One. Find your shadow agents first, then run the three-question audit. Every security team I work with assumes the AI agents in their environment are the ones IT provisioned. They are not. The agents that will cause your first agent-driven incident are the ones individual employees set up last quarter through a SaaS integration the security team has never reviewed — OAuth grants from personal accounts, AI features inside SaaS tools you already pay for, browser extensions that interact with internal systems. Once you have that visibility, run a three-question audit against every agent in scope: who authorized this agent, what is it allowed to do on whose behalf, where is the audit trail of what it has done. Most organizations cannot answer all three for the majority of agents in their environment. Until they can, every subsequent control they build is built on a foundation of optimism.

Two. Move toward an agent-distinct identity tier. Most organizations are treating agents as if they were unusual users — slotting them into the existing identity infrastructure with an OAuth token and trusting the audit trail to sort things out later. That works until it does not. The architecture that will hold up is one in which agents have a distinct authentication category, with their own token format, their own audit logs, and their own revocation flows. Every action an agent takes on a user's behalf should be cryptographically attributable to "this agent, on this date, with this delegation scope," not just "this user." When something goes wrong, you need to be able to tell the difference between the user acting and the agent acting in seconds, not weeks.

Three. Write the accountability policy before the incident. Decide, in writing, who is accountable for what kinds of agent actions, in what jurisdictions, with what reporting requirements. Get general counsel or your equivalent legal advisor involved. Do this before the first agent-driven incident, not after, because the after-incident version of this conversation is much more expensive.

What the body knew

When I played basketball at the highest level I ever reached — wearing Nigeria's jersey at the FIBA Africa Championship for Women in 2007, the qualifier for the Beijing 2008 Olympics — every action I took on the floor was attributable to me. The system that authenticated me as a player was the roster, the federation, the line in the program. The system that identified me as a player was my body, my game, the specific way I read the floor under pressure. A teammate could not take a free throw in my name. An opposing scout could not credit my rebounds to someone else. The cryptography of sport is thin. The accountability is total.

The new AI agent infrastructure is asking us to invert that relationship. The cryptography is getting stronger every quarter. The accountability is going the other way — diffused across agents, vendors, model providers, and the human whose name happens to be on the action. It will work, mostly. The places where it does not work are where the breaches of the next decade will happen. The job of the security architect, right now, is to make sure the accountability does not get any thinner before the cryptography catches up to where it needs to be.
