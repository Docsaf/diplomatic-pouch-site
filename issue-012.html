The Half-Life of Mastery

On twelve years of federal security work and what I learned, each time I had to let a skill go.

The first time I had to put down a skill I had worked hard to earn was at NASA, around 2015. I had spent most of two years becoming genuinely competent at the on-premises security operations stack we had standardized on. I knew the sensors, the correlation rules, the quirks of our SIEM, the behavior of our firewall policy engine under load. I could tell you from memory which log fields got dropped when the queue filled and which detections would silently stop firing when the identity source got rate-limited. That kind of knowledge is expensive. You buy it with late nights and bad weekends and an entire social life you did not get to have while you were building muscle memory on a vendor's console.

Then the agency announced the cloud migration.

I remember the moment I understood what the announcement meant. I was sitting in a program review with a slide deck in front of me that laid out a two-year roadmap, and I realized that approximately seventy percent of what I had just finished mastering was now a legacy skill. Not useless. Just no longer the thing that mattered. The thing that would matter two years from then was cloud-native security architecture, which I did not yet know, and which no one in the room fully knew yet, because the shape of it was still being invented by the industry while we were being asked to design it for a federal mission.

The feeling in my chest was not professional. It was personal. The thing I knew, the thing I had become because of the knowing, was being deprecated in a slide deck. I had not asked to be deprecated. I had been doing a good job.

The pattern I did not yet see

I handled that first deprecation poorly. I did what people do when they are afraid: I overworked. I treated the cloud learning curve as an emergency and I attacked it the way I had been taught to attack things in sports — more reps, more hours, more intensity. I burned out somewhere around month nine. The burnout was not that I was bad at the new material. The burnout was that I was approaching the new material as though it were an existential threat rather than a professional cycle. I thought if I did not master cloud security fast enough, I would be left behind permanently. So I rushed, and the rushing produced panic, and the panic produced exhaustion, and the exhaustion produced the exact stall I had been trying to avoid.

I did not know yet that this was a cycle. I thought it was a one-time catastrophe. The realization that it was a cycle took three more rounds.

At DOJ, four years later, it happened again. The specific technology changed — this time it was the shift from traditional security operations to a more orchestrated, API-driven, detection-engineering model, with heavy investment in automation that frankly did not exist at the maturity level the vendors claimed. The emotional shape was identical. I had spent a chunk of my cloud transition rebuilding mastery, and now a new wave was asking me to put down what I had just picked up and reach for something that was still being built while I was being asked to use it.

At Slack, during and after the Salesforce acquisition, it happened a third time, in a different register. The technology stayed roughly stable but the operational model collapsed and re-formed around enterprise integration patterns that were foreign to how the company had grown up. The muscle memory of what it meant to be a Slack security engineer under Slack governance was no longer the muscle memory of what it meant to be a Slack security engineer under Salesforce governance. The deprecation this time was not of my technical knowledge. It was of my organizational knowledge, which had been just as expensive to earn and was being written off just as quickly.

At Sand, as the first security hire for a global data and AI company moving through agentic systems and Claude enterprise deployment on an aggressive timeline, I am inside the fourth cycle now. The specific thing being deprecated this time is the assumption that security review happens before deployment. Agentic AI compresses that interval to zero in a way that invalidates most of what I know about how security programs sit inside release processes. The pattern is familiar even though the content is new.

What the cycles actually taught me

The first thing the cycles taught me is that mastery has a half-life, and in cybersecurity the half-life is shorter than most practitioners want to believe. The specific tool, the specific framework, the specific runbook you spent two years learning — half of what makes that mastery useful will be stale within three to five years. Some of it faster. The acceleration since 2020 has been real; the AI-era acceleration since 2024 has been qualitatively different. If you tie your professional identity to the specific stack you currently know, you have given yourself a short career on purpose.

The second thing the cycles taught me is that the mastery is not actually what you were building. What you were building was the capacity to become masterful. The specific firewall policy engine I knew at NASA is gone. The agency has moved on from that vendor. The three cloud architectures I rebuilt proficiency in across subsequent years are also, in various ways, obsolete or in transition. What remains, and what has compounded, is my capacity to walk into a new control plane, read the documentation at the right level of abstraction, identify the three concepts I actually need to internalize, and reach working proficiency inside a few weeks rather than a few years. That capacity is the asset. The specific knowledge is a byproduct.

The specific knowledge is a byproduct. The capacity to acquire it is the career.

The third thing is harder to say because it is less flattering. The cycles taught me that my worst instinct — the one that said I had to catch up or be left behind — was actively destructive. The practitioners I have watched handle these transitions well do not treat them as emergencies. They treat them as the normal operating condition of the field. They allocate a protected block of time each week to learning the new thing. They refuse to let the learning expand into panic hours. They accept that for the first six to twelve months of a cycle, their output on the new stack will look worse than their output on the old stack. They do not treat this temporary dip as a crisis. They treat it as the expected cost of staying in the game.

The practitioners who treat the transitions as emergencies are the ones who burn out, as I did at NASA. The practitioners who treat the transitions as inevitable are the ones who are still in the field twenty years later, calmly building the next thing.

The thing that does not deprecate

What has not changed across any of the four cycles, and will not change in the fifth or sixth, is the fundamental structure of what cybersecurity practice actually is. Cybersecurity is a trust practice. It is the discipline of ensuring that information systems do what we expect them to do, for the people we expect them to protect, in the conditions we expect them to operate under. The specific controls change. The specific tools change. The threat landscape changes. The structure of the question does not. The question is always: who is this system for, what is it supposed to do, and what happens when it does something else?

Every one of the four cycles I have lived through was a change in the surface of the answer, not a change in the question. Once I understood that, the cycles stopped feeling existential. They started feeling like weather. You do not treat the arrival of winter as a personal catastrophe. You put on different clothes. You notice that your routines shift. You accept that the shape of each day is now a little different for a few months, and then it shifts again.

The practitioners I now mentor at Hacking the Workforce — the apprentices just coming into the field, often from career-change backgrounds, often from backgrounds historically excluded from this industry — ask me what skill they should bet on. They are asking the wrong question, and it is not their fault. They have been told by the industry that if they pick the right certification, the right language, the right framework, they will have a career. They will not. The certification will be stale in four years. The language will not be hot in six. The framework will have been replaced by a successor whose job description reads like a parody of the original.

What I tell them is this. The thing you are building is not a stack of knowledge. The thing you are building is a self that is capable of acquiring the next stack, and then the one after that, and then the one after that, for forty years. Treat your learning capacity as an asset to be sustained, not a resource to be burned. Protect the hours you spend becoming competent at new things from the panic you will feel when you realize another deprecation is coming. The panic will come. You get to decide whether you let it run your schedule.

The dispatch I would send to my twenty-nine-year-old self

If I could send a dispatch back to the version of me who sat in that NASA program review in 2015 and watched his mastery get deprecated in a slide deck, this is what it would say. The thing you are feeling right now is not a sign that you are falling behind. It is a sign that you have joined a profession that will ask this of you every three to five years for the rest of your working life. You can treat that fact as cruel, in which case you will burn out and leave the field within a decade, as roughly half of your cohort will. Or you can treat it as the terms of the deal, in which case you will still be here in twenty years, calmly building the thing that comes after the thing we have not yet started building.

You do not have to earn your right to stay in the field by killing yourself every time the field changes shape. The field will change shape with or without your sacrifice. Your sacrifice does not make the change easier. It just makes you worse at being alive while the change is happening. Save your capacity. Spend it in blocks you choose. Trust that the practitioner you are building will be a more valuable asset at forty-five than the specific skills you were so afraid to lose at twenty-nine.

The mastery was never the point. The capacity to acquire it was. Protect the capacity. Everything else gets rebuilt.
