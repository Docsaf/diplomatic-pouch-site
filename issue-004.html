What the CIA Triad Was Never Designed to Protect

A doctoral argument made plain — and the case for an expanded foundation of cybersecurity practice.

The CIA Triad — Confidentiality, Integrity, Availability — is the foundation of cybersecurity practice. It is taught in every introductory course, referenced in every framework, and applied, well or poorly, in every security program of any real maturity. It works. It is also, in a way the field has been slow to name, incomplete.

The Triad protects information. It was not designed to protect people. And the gap between those two things — between the integrity of data and the integrity of the human at the end of the data — is where the next decade of cybersecurity practice is going to be made or lost.

I want to make a careful argument for that claim, because it is easy to mistake for something it is not.

What the Triad does well

The Triad is a discipline. Confidentiality says: information should only be accessible to those authorized. Integrity says: information should not be altered except by authorized actors. Availability says: information should be accessible when needed. These are foundational protections. The field has spent fifty years building the cryptographic, architectural, and operational machinery to deliver on them. Most of the time, most of the machinery works.

I am not arguing against the Triad. I am arguing that the Triad is necessary and not sufficient — and that the field's overinvestment in the Triad as the whole frame, rather than as the foundation under a larger frame, has produced a generation of security programs that protect data exquisitely while leaving the humans on the other side of the data largely exposed.

Where the Triad runs out

My doctoral research at Marymount University, completed in 2023 under the title Towards a More Equitable Digital Future, examined the relationship between demographic factors, intersecting marginalized identities, digital trust, and smartphone usage. I will spare you the methodology section. The finding I want to put in front of you is this:

Digital trust varies systematically along lines of intersecting marginalized identity. The data are not subtle.

Trans American adults report markedly lower smartphone ownership than cisgender adults. Black and Hispanic Americans are significantly more likely than white Americans to rely on smartphones as their sole device for internet access. Adults in households earning under the federal poverty line are far less likely to have home broadband and far more likely to be funneled into platforms whose security architecture was not designed with their threat model in mind.

These are not interesting data points only because they describe inequality. They are interesting because they describe a population of users for whom the Triad, applied competently and in good faith, still produces a digital environment they cannot trust. The system is doing what the Triad asks of it. The humans are not protected. The gap is where the people live.

The privacy paradox is not what we have said it is

Security and privacy researchers have for years described what we call the privacy paradox: people say they care about privacy but behave in ways that undermine it. They share location data. They click through consent flows. They use platforms that they know are harvesting them. The standard interpretation is that users are inconsistent, irrational, or insufficiently educated.

My research, and the research of many others I cite in my dissertation, points at a different interpretation. The paradox is not in the user. The paradox is in the design. People whose threat model has been shaped by historical experience of surveillance, discrimination, and exclusion behave rationally inside systems that were not built for them. Their behavior looks paradoxical only when measured against the assumption of an "ideal hyper-informed user" whose threat model matches the one the system was designed for.

When a Black user in a low-income household uses a smartphone as their sole internet device, downloads an app with permissive default sharing, and clicks through the consent flow — they are not being irrational. They are being rational inside an environment that has historically extracted from them, where their alternatives are constrained by income, and where their threat model has been calibrated by experience the platform designer never considered. The Triad protects the integrity of that user's data inside the platform's architecture. It does not protect the user from the architecture itself.

What an expanded foundation would look like

I am not proposing we replace the Triad. I am proposing we extend it. The foundational triad of confidentiality, integrity, and availability needs to sit underneath a second triad that protects people, not just information. I will sketch what I think those three properties are. The full version is in the dissertation; the public-facing version is below.

Reliability across difference. The system must behave as expected for users whose context, identity, and threat model differ from the designer's default assumptions. Not just on average, but on the margins. A facial recognition system that performs at high accuracy in aggregate but at sharply lower accuracy for dark-skinned women is not a reliable system. It is a system that has decided whose reliability counts.

Equitable security. The protective capacity of the system must be available to all users, not concentrated among those who already have the resources to use the system safely. A recovery flow that requires a verified email, a phone number not shared with anyone, and a credit card on file is a recovery flow that has decided whose accounts are worth recovering.

Privacy as practice. Privacy cannot be designed as a checkbox in a consent flow and then forgotten. It must be a continuous architectural practice that takes seriously the asymmetric vulnerability of users whose identity, location, or political circumstance makes the cost of a data leak higher than the platform's incident response framework was designed to handle. A platform that treats every user's data leak as equivalent is a platform that has not been paying attention.

These three properties — reliability across difference, equitable security, privacy as practice — are not in tension with the Triad. They are what the Triad asks of us when we take seriously the question of whom we are protecting and why.

What this means for practice

If you run a security program, here is what I would ask you to consider this week.

Audit your incident response framework for asymmetric harm. When a user's data is exposed in your environment, does your IR plan treat all users the same? Or does it recognize that some users — domestic violence survivors, asylum seekers, trans users in hostile jurisdictions, journalists, political dissidents — face categorically higher cost from the same incident? If your IR plan does not have a flag for asymmetric harm, your IR plan is operating at a coarser resolution than the population you serve.

Look at your identity layer through the lens of equity, not just the lens of authentication strength. Who gets locked out of your recovery flows? Who is more likely to be flagged as anomalous in your fraud-detection system? Whose access patterns are categorized as suspicious because they don't match the modal user your detection model was trained on? These are security questions. They are also equity questions. They are the same question.

Hire and listen to security practitioners whose lived experience extends your team's threat model. This is not a diversity initiative. It is a security practice. The threats your team cannot see are the threats your team's composition has not equipped it to see. The threats your team cannot model are the threats your team has not lived close enough to. Build the team that can see what your current team cannot. Then listen to them when they speak.

The pouch

A diplomatic pouch is a sealed container that crosses borders untouched because the holder has been vetted, the contents are protected by treaty, and the trust has been built in advance. The pouch carries what the institution behind it has decided is worth protecting.

We are, all of us, deciding what the cybersecurity field thinks is worth protecting. The Triad has carried us this far. The next iteration of practice has to decide whether the pouch is large enough to carry the people the field has historically left exposed.

I am writing this newsletter, in part, to make the argument that it can be. And to do my small part in extending the seal of the pouch to the people who have been outside it for too long.
