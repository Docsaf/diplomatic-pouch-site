By Dr. Safi Mojidi · Cable · ~8 min read

The Sender Field

Why the identity layer has always been the hardest part — and why getting it right matters more in 2026 than it ever has.

In 2004, the daily USAID notice I wrote each morning was authenticated by exactly one thing: the From field in an email. The Global Address List vouched for me. Foreign Service officers around the world trusted the dispatch because the system told them it came from a verified sender. The cable was real because the GAL said it was real.

Then they replied to my mother.

Not because the system failed. The system worked perfectly. Two correctly authenticated Mojidis appeared in the Global Address List, and the human readers — busy, on multiple time zones, scanning at speed — collapsed them into one. The system had done its job. The humans had not.

I have thought about this single piece of accidental security training more times than any class I took in my doctoral program. It is, I have come to believe, the most important thing I know about how systems actually fail.

The gap

The identity layer in modern security is the hardest layer to build because it is the layer where systems and humans negotiate. Systems are good at authentication: cryptographically proving that the sender is who they claim to be. Humans are good at identification: knowing who someone is by context, history, voice, and pattern. The two are not the same thing. They have never been the same thing. The failure mode of every major identity breach in the last decade has been the gap between them.

When attackers run an MFA fatigue campaign, they are not breaking authentication. They are exploiting the gap between authentication and identification. The system correctly authenticated the user's session; the human pressed Approve out of fatigue. When attackers run a deepfake voice call to authorize a wire transfer, they are not breaking authentication. The CFO authorizing the wire correctly identified the CEO's voice. The identification was real. The voice was synthetic. The gap was where the attack lived.

When a senior diplomat replied to my mother thinking she was the sender, no authentication failed. The GAL was right. The diplomat was wrong, in a way that no technical control could correct, because the failure was inside the human reader's expectation of who Mojidi could plausibly be.

Twenty years of stronger authentication

The technology industry has spent the last two decades trying to close the gap by making authentication stronger. Passwords became MFA. MFA became hardware keys. Hardware keys became passkeys. Each generation has been a real improvement in authentication. None of them have addressed identification, because identification is a human practice and humans do not patch on a release cadence.

I want to say this clearly because the security industry rarely does: we have spent twenty years optimizing the wrong half of the problem. The authentication half is solvable, and we are close to having largely solved it. The identification half is the half that still costs organizations breach after breach, and we have barely begun to take it seriously as an engineering problem.

Three things I tell my consulting clients

When a security leader brings me in for a strategic engagement around identity, I tell them three things that almost always reframe how their team is approaching the work.

One. Stop measuring identity maturity by authentication strength alone. If your only metric is "percentage of accounts on hardware keys," you are measuring half the problem. Add a metric for identification practice — how often your humans correctly identify the people in their flows, particularly in high-value transactions. Phishing simulation data is part of this measurement, but only part. Voice-call verification rates for wire authorizations, deepfake awareness in vendor onboarding, and the actual cadence of out-of-band confirmation for sensitive actions all belong in the same dashboard. If your CISO dashboard shows 99% MFA adoption but cannot tell you how many wire authorizations were verified out-of-band last quarter, you have an authentication dashboard, not an identity dashboard.

Two. Build for the wrong human, not the right one. Every identity system I have seen fail at scale was designed for the human who reads carefully, pauses before clicking, and verifies the sender before acting. That human does not exist in volume. Real humans are tired, are doing six things at once, are responding from their phone between meetings, and are pattern-matching the surname Mojidi to the only Mojidi they have ever met. Design your identity flows for that human. Make the system carry the weight that the human cannot carry. If your authorization flow requires the human to notice that a sender field is subtly off, your flow is broken, regardless of how strong the cryptography is.

Three. The identity layer is where equity work happens. This is the piece most security organizations are missing, and it is the piece that will increasingly matter as regulation catches up. The people most likely to be misidentified by your systems, most likely to be flagged as anomalous, most likely to be locked out of recovery flows, and most likely to be targeted by social engineering that exploits cultural assumptions are the people whose identities sit at the margins of how your systems were originally designed. This is not a diversity-and-inclusion concern bolted onto the security program. It is a security concern. The bias in your identity layer is a vulnerability in your identity layer. I will write more about this in a coming Treaty.

What the cable taught me

The daily USAID notice taught me that the sender field is sacred and insufficient. That the strongest authentication in the world only narrows the attack surface; it does not eliminate it. That every system, no matter how technically rigorous, eventually meets a tired human who is going to make a reasonable assumption that turns out to be wrong. The job of the security architect is to anticipate that meeting and design for it.

My mother never minded receiving the misaddressed replies. She would forward them back to me with a one-line correction, and I would respond with the careful subject-line clarification of a twenty-two-year-old learning what it means to share a name with someone whose reputation is the actual asset under management. The system worked. The humans were doing their best. The gap between them was where I started to learn what the work actually was.
